[SAM]: Got it. But what’s the first thing I should focus on? The "rulebook" for AI agents?

[ALEX]: Start with context windows. Imagine you’re a chatbot. Your "memory" is limited—you can only remember the last few messages. That’s your context window. Too small, and you forget what the user said. Too large, and you get slow and expensive.

[SAM]: So it’s like a sliding scale between memory and speed? How do I find the sweet spot?

[ALEX]: It depends on the task. For a customer service bot, you might prioritize recency—keeping the last 5 messages. For summarizing a book, you’d need a wider window. But here’s the catch: most models have *token limits*.

[SAM]: Tokens? Like cryptocurrency?

[ALEX]: Nope! Tokens are chunks of text—words, punctuation, even parts of words. For example, "knowledge" might be one token, but "KnowledgeBase" could be two. Models have a max token limit, say 1,024. Exceed it, and the model cuts you off mid-sentence.

[SAM]: That’s frustrating! So if I’m feeding a model a long document, it’s like trying to read a book through a keyhole?

[ALEX]: Precisely. That’s why chunking matters—breaking text into digestible pieces. But even then, you need to manage the context window. For example, if you’re analyzing a legal contract, you might prioritize clauses over boilerplate.

[SAM]: What if the model needs to connect ideas across chunks? Like remembering a definition from page 1 when analyzing page 10?

[SAM]: Okay, but what about the "why"? Why does a model even *have* these limits?

[ALEX]: Two reasons: computational cost and attention mechanisms. Transformers—the backbone of most AI models—use self-attention, which scales quadratically with input size. Double the tokens, quadruple the compute. It’s like adding more floors to a building—each level needs stronger supports.

[ALEX]: Not always. Even with infinite compute, you’d hit diminishing returns. The human brain doesn’t store every detail of a conversation either—it focuses on salient points. AI agents need the same discipline.

[ALEX]: Bingo. That’s deliberate practice—teaching the model to prioritize, not just process. Start small, understand the limits, then scale intelligently.
