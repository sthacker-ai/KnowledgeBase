[ALEX]: The ADR% scale splits volatility into four zones: 0–1.5% is "too low," 1.5–3% is "ideal," 3–5% is "high," and above 5% is "excessive." But why does a 1.5% daily range suddenly become "ideal" for swing trading?

[SAM]: Is this threshold arbitrary, or is there a statistical basis for it?

[ALEX]: It’s based on practical experience—most swing trades aim for 5–10% moves over days or weeks. If a stock’s daily range is only 1%, it would take 10+ days just to hit a 10% target, which is inefficient. But if it’s 3%, you might see that move in 3–5 days, which aligns better with typical holding periods.

[SAM]: So it’s about time efficiency. But what if a stock has an ADR% of 2.5%—right in the middle of the "ideal" zone. How do you decide whether to trade it?

[ALEX]: That’s where context matters. A 2.5% ADR% is solid, but you’d still check other factors: Is it trending? Is volume supporting the move? Is it near a key level? The ADR% filter narrows your universe, but it’s not the only criterion.

[SAM]: What about the "high" zone (3–5%)? Is that still tradable, or is it too risky?

[ALEX]: It’s tradable, but the risk-reward dynamics change. A 4% ADR% stock might hit your 10% target in just 2–3 days, but it could also reverse just as quickly. You’d need tighter stops and faster exits. Some traders prefer to fade these—selling rallies or buying dips—rather than holding through volatility.

[SAM]: And the "excessive" zone (>5%)? Why avoid it entirely?

[ALEX]: Because the noise overwhelms the signal. At 6%+ ADR%, you’re dealing with extreme intraday whipsaws. Even if the trend is strong, the randomness makes it hard to time entries and exits. It’s better suited for day traders, not swing traders.

[SAM]: How do you handle ADR% for lower-priced stocks? Penny stocks often have huge percentage moves but tiny absolute ranges.

[ALEX]: That’s why ADR% is better than raw ADR—it normalizes for price. A ₹10 stock with a 5% ADR% (₹0.50 range) is just as volatile as a ₹1,000 stock with a 5% ADR% (₹50 range). The key is consistency: if a stock’s ADR% is stable, you can rely on it.

[SAM]: What if ADR% changes suddenly? For example, a stock jumps from 1.8% to 4% overnight. How do you adjust?

[ALEX]: That’s a red flag. Sudden spikes often mean a breakout or breakdown, but they can also signal exhaustion. You’d want to see if volume confirms the move and whether the trend aligns with your strategy. If not, it might be better to wait for stabilization.

[SAM]: Can you integrate ADR% with other indicators, like RSI or moving averages?

[ALEX]: Absolutely. For example, you might only take long positions in the "ideal" ADR% zone when the stock is above its 20-day moving average and RSI is above 50. This stacks the odds in your favor by combining volatility, trend, and momentum.

[SAM]: What’s the biggest mistake traders make with ADR%?

[ALEX]: Overfitting. Some traders tweak the thresholds to match past performance, but the zones are meant as guidelines, not rigid rules. The market evolves, and so should your approach. Use ADR% as a starting point, not a crutch.

[SAM]: How do you backtest an ADR%-based strategy?

[ALEX]: Start by filtering stocks into the four zones, then apply your entry/exit rules to each group. Compare performance: does the "ideal" zone really outperform? If not, adjust your thresholds or strategy. Most platforms let you automate this with custom scans.

[SAM]: Final question: if ADR% is so useful, why don’t more traders use it?

[ALEX]: Because it’s not a standalone system—it’s a filter. Traders often chase the next "holy grail" indicator, but ADR% is just one piece of the puzzle. The real skill is combining it with other tools and adapting to market conditions.
