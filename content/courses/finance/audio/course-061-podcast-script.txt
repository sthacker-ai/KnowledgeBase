[SAM]: What exactly do you mean by “base” in this context, and why does its absence hurt the trade?  
[ALEX]: A base is a consolidation zone where the price moves sideways after the EMA crossover, reflecting balanced supply and demand; if you buy before the base completes, you risk buying into a drift or reversal, which widens your stop‑loss and shrinks potential profit.  
[SAM]: Can you walk me through a concrete example of a valid base forming after a crossover?  
[ALEX]: In Laurus Labs, after the March 12 crossover the stock traded between ₹1 020 and ₹1 045 for six sessions, with volume averaging 1.2 × the 20‑day average, showing a tight range and low volatility—this sideways box is the base.  
[SAM]: Once the base is identified, what constitutes a breakout that warrants entry, and how do you confirm it with volume?  
[ALEX]: A breakout occurs when the price closes above the upper boundary of the base on a candle whose volume is at least 1.5 × the 20‑day average; that volume surge signals institutional participation and validates the move.  
[SAM]: Where would you place the stop‑loss for that breakout entry, and how do you determine the target?  
[ALEX]: The stop‑loss goes just below the lowest point of the base; the target is often set using a measured‑move projection—take the height of the base and add it to the breakout point for a first profit level.  
[SAM]: If the initial breakout is missed, the strategy mentions a pullback to the 21‑EMA forming a cup shape; how does that work?  
[ALEX]: After a missed breakout, the price often retraces to the 21‑EMA, creating a rounded bottom (the cup); when the price then closes above the cup’s resistance line on high volume, it offers a second entry opportunity with a similar stop‑loss placement below the cup low.  
[SAM]: For that cup‑shape entry, what stop‑loss and target rules do you apply, and how does the risk‑to‑reward compare to the first breakout?  
[ALEX]: Stop‑loss is placed beneath the cup’s lowest point; target uses the cup’s depth measured from the breakout point, usually yielding a risk‑to‑reward of at least 1:2, comparable to the base‑breakout trade when volume confirms.

[ALEX]: In choppy, low‑volume markets the EMA crossover whipsaws; filtering by requiring a minimum base width (at least 3% of price) and a volume breakout threshold reduces false entries, and avoiding stocks under ₹50 helps maintain reliable volume data.
