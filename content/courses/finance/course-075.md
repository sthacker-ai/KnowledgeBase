## Core Concepts  

### Market Breadth  
Market breadth measures the number of participating stocks versus those that are lagging, providing a gauge of underlying market strength or weakness beyond the movement of a few large‑cap indices. Common breadth indicators include the Advance‑Decline Line (A/D Line), the McClellan Oscillator, the percentage of stocks above their 50‑day moving average, and the New Highs‑New Lows ratio. When breadth is strong, a rising index is supported by broad participation, suggesting the trend is likely to persist. Conversely, deteriorating breadth—where advances fall while the index still climbs—can warn of hidden weakness and impending reversals. In swing trading, you would first check the daily A/D Line: if it is making higher highs alongside the index, you have a bullish environment conducive to long swing trades; if the A/D Line diverges downward, you may favor short‑biased setups or reduce position size. Breadth can also be applied sector‑wise: calculating the proportion of advancing stocks within a sector helps confirm whether rotation into that sector is genuine or merely a few leaders driving the move.

### Sector & Stock Rotation (RRG)  
Relative Rotation Graphs (RRG), developed by Julius de Kempenaer, visualize the relative strength and momentum of a group of securities (typically sectors) against a benchmark and each other. The RRG plot places each sector on a two‑dimensional plane: the X‑axis shows relative strength (RS) compared to the benchmark, while the Y‑axis shows relative momentum (the rate of change of RS). Sectors move through four quadrants: leading (strong RS and improving momentum), weakening (strong RS but deteriorating momentum), lagging (weak RS and deteriorating momentum), and improving (weak RS but improving momentum). By watching a sector’s trajectory, you can anticipate where capital will flow next. For swing trading, you might start with a broad market scan, identify sectors in the “leading” quadrant, then drill down to individual stocks within those sectors that also show favorable RS and momentum. When a sector transitions from “improving” to “leading,” it often signals the start of a multi‑week uptrend, providing a thematic backdrop for long swing trades. Conversely, a sector moving from “leading” to “weakening” suggests profit‑taking and may prompt you to exit longs or consider shorts.

### Chartink/TradingView Screeners  
Screeners automate the process of filtering thousands of securities based on user‑defined criteria, turning a manual scan into a rapid, repeatable workflow. Chartink offers a web‑based screener with a formula language similar to Excel, allowing conditions such as `close > sma(close,20) AND rsi(14) > 60 AND volume > avg(volume,20)*1.5`. TradingView’s screener uses its own Pine Script‑like syntax for alerts and also provides a visual builder where you can drag‑and‑drop indicators (e.g., MACD, Bollinger Bands) and set thresholds. A typical swing‑trading screener might combine: (1) price above the 50‑day EMA (trend filter), (2) relative strength > 70 versus the S&P 500 (sector‑relative strength), (3) recent breakout above a 20‑day high with volume > 1.5× average (breakout filter), and (4) RSI between 50 and 70 (momentum not overextended). Once the screener returns a list, you can export the symbols to a watchlist for further chart analysis. Both platforms allow you to save screener templates, schedule automatic runs (e.g., pre‑market), and receive email or push notifications when new symbols meet the criteria, ensuring you never miss a setup.

### Breakouts  
A breakout occurs when price moves decisively through a defined level of support or resistance, often accompanied by an expansion in volume, signaling that supply‑demand balance has shifted. In swing trading, breakouts are prized because they can launch sustained moves that capture multiple days of profit. Key breakout patterns include:  
- **Classic horizontal breakout**: price clears a prior swing high (resistance) or swing low (support).  
- **Trendline breakout**: price pierces an ascending or descending trendline drawn from recent swing points.  
- **Chart pattern breakout**: e.g., ascending triangle, cup‑and‑handle, or flag/pennant resolution.  
Valid breakouts usually show: (a) a close beyond the level (not just an intraday spike), (b) volume at least 1.5‑2× the average of the prior 10‑20 bars, and (c) follow‑through price action in the direction of the break within the next 1‑3 bars. Traders often place a stop just below the breakout point (for longs) or above it (for shorts) and target a profit based on the height of the preceding consolidation or a measured move from the pattern.

### VCP (Volatility Contraction Pattern)  
The Volatility Contraction Pattern, popularized by Mark Minervini, identifies stocks that have experienced a series of tightening price ranges (lower volatility) followed by a sudden expansion on high volume—a sign that accumulated energy is about to be released. A typical VCP shows:  
1. **Prior uptrend**: the stock is in a clear upward trend (price above rising 50‑day MA).  
2. **Three or more contractions**: each successive pullback makes a higher low and a narrower price range than the previous one, indicating decreasing selling pressure.  
3. **Breakout**: price bursts above the highest point of the last contraction with volume significantly above average.  
The pattern reflects a shift from distribution to accumulation, often preceding a strong continuation move. In practice, you would first ensure the stock is in a strong sector (via RRG) and passes a breadth filter, then look for the VCP on the daily chart. Entry is taken on the breakout bar’s close, with a stop placed below the low of the deepest contraction bar. Targets can be set using a multiple of the contraction width (e.g., 2× the narrowest range) or a measured move from the base of the pattern.

### Darvas Box  
The Darvas Box method, devised by Nicolas Darvas in the 1950s, isolates stocks that are trading within a defined rectangular range (the “box”) and then break out of that range on increased volume. To construct a Darvas Box:  
- Identify a period where the stock makes a series of higher highs and higher lows, forming a clear upward‑sloping channel.  
- Draw a horizontal line at the recent high (top of the box) and another at the recent low (bottom).  
- As long as price stays within the box, you consider the stock in a consolidation phase.  
- When price closes above the top of the box with volume greater than the average of the prior 3‑5 days, you have a Darvas breakout signal.  
The method emphasizes buying only when the stock is making new highs within an overall uptrend, thus aligning with the trend‑following nature of swing trading. Stops are placed just below the box’s bottom (or below the breakout candle’s low) and targets are often set at a multiple of the box height (e.g., 2× the box height) or at the next logical resistance level.

### Momentum & EMA Setups  
Momentum‑based swing trades capitalize on the persistence of price direction. Exponential Moving Averages (EMAs) give more weight to recent prices, making them responsive to short‑term shifts while still smoothing noise. Common EMA‑based setups include:  
- **EMA crossover**: a fast EMA (e.g., 9‑period) crossing above a slower EMA (e.g., 21‑period) signals bullish momentum; the opposite crossover signals bearish momentum.  
- **EMA pullback**: price retraces to touch or slightly penetrate a key EMA (often the 20‑ or 50‑day) during an uptrend, offering a low‑risk entry aligned with the prevailing trend.  
- **EMA slope + price position**: the EMA itself is rising (positive slope) and price trades above it, reinforcing bullish bias.  
To increase reliability, traders often combine EMA signals with momentum oscillators such as the Relative Strength Index (RSI) or Stochastic. For example, a long setup might require: price > 50‑day EMA, 9‑EMA crossing above 21‑EMA, RSI between 50 and 70 (showing strength without overbought conditions), and volume above average on the crossover bar. Stops are placed below the recent swing low or below the EMA that acted as support, while targets can be based on a fixed risk‑reward ratio (e.g., 1:2) or the distance to the next resistance level identified via prior swing highs or Fibonacci extensions.

### Risk Management  
Effective risk management transforms a series of trades into a sustainable equity curve. Core components taught in the course include:  
- **Position sizing**: determine the dollar amount to risk per trade (commonly 1‑2% of total equity). Calculate shares = (equity × risk %)/(entry price − stop price).  
- **Stop‑loss placement**: use technical stops (below swing low, below EMA, or below breakout level) rather than arbitrary percentages, ensuring the stop respects market structure.  
- **Profit targets**: set based on measured moves, pattern projections, or a fixed risk‑reward ratio; consider scaling out (taking partial profits at predefined levels) to lock in gains while letting a portion run.  
- **Trade‑level metrics**: track win rate, average win, average loss, and expectancy (Expectancy = (win % × avg win) − (loss % × avg loss)). Aim for a positive expectancy.  
- **Portfolio‑level controls**: limit sector exposure (e.g., no more than 30% of capital in any one sector), enforce a maximum daily loss threshold, and avoid overtrading by setting a maximum number of concurrent positions.  
- **Psychological rules**: adhere to the plan, avoid moving stops to “give the trade more room,” and review each trade post‑mortem to refine the process.  
By integrating these rules with the technical setups described above, you create a framework where each trade has a defined risk, a clear edge, and a logical exit plan.

## How It Works / Step‑by‑Step  
Below is a detailed workflow that strings together the concepts from the course into a repeatable swing‑trading process. Feel free to adapt the exact parameters to your instrument, time frame, and risk tolerance.

1. **Macro‑Breadth Check (Daily)**  
   - Open a chart of a broad market index (e.g., S&P 500).  
   - Add the Advance‑Decline Line (A/D Line) and the percentage of stocks above their 50‑day MA.  
   - If the A/D Line is making higher highs and the % above 50‑day MA > 60%, consider the environment bullish for longs; if deteriorating, shift to a neutral or short‑biased stance.  

2. **Sector Rotation Scan (RRG)**  
   - Load an RRG chart with the major sectors (XLY, XLP, XLE, XLF, XLV, XLI, XLB, XLRE, XLK, XLC) against the S&P 500.  
   - Identify sectors residing in the “leading” quadrant (strong RS, improving momentum).  
   - Note any sector transitioning from “improving” to “leading” as a potential early‑entry opportunity.  

3. **Screener Build (Chartink/TradingView)**  
   - In Chartink, create a new screener with the following pseudo‑code:  
     ```
     close > ema(close,50)   // trend filter  
     AND rsi(14) > 50 AND rsi(14) < 70   // momentum not extreme  
     AND volume > sma(volume,20)*1.5   // volume confirmation  
     AND close > high[20]   // breakout above 20‑day high  
     AND sector in ["XLK","XLV","XLY"]   // limit to leading sectors from RRG  
     ```  
   - In TradingView, use the visual screener: add EMA(50), RSI(14), Volume SMA(20), and a price > highest(high,20) condition, then filter by sector.  
   - Save the screener and run it pre‑market; export the resulting tickers to a watchlist.  

4. **Pattern Validation (VCP/Darvas/Breakout)**  
   - For each ticker, open the daily chart.  
   - **VCP check**: look for at least three successive contractions with higher lows and narrowing ranges, then a breakout candle closing above the prior contraction high with volume > 1.5× average.  
   - **Darvas check**: identify a clear box formed by a series of higher highs and higher lows; wait for a close above the box top with elevated volume.  
   - **Generic breakout**: if neither VCP nor Darvas is evident, look for a clean horizontal breakout above a prior swing high or trendline breakout with volume confirmation.  
   - If a setup passes, note the entry price (close of breakout bar), stop level (below the low of the breakout bar or below the box/contraction low), and initial target (e.g., 2× risk).  

5. **Momentum/EMA Confirmation**  
   - Add 9‑EMA and 21‑EMA to the chart.  
   - Confirm that the 9‑EMA has crossed above the 21‑EMA on or before the breakout bar (or that price is above both EMAs with the 9‑EMA rising).  
   - Verify RSI(14) is between 50 and 70 (avoid buying into overbought territory).  
   - If all align, proceed to trade; if not, wait for a better‑timed entry or discard the setup.  

6. **Order Execution & Risk Management**  
   - Calculate position size:  
     ```
     equity = $100,000  
     risk_per_trade = 1% = $1,000  
     stop_distance = entry_price - stop_price  
     shares = floor($1,000 / stop_distance)  
     ```  
   - Place a bracket order: entry at market (or limit slightly below the breakout close to avoid slippage), stop loss at the predetermined level, and a profit‑target order at the target level.  
   - Consider scaling out: sell 50% of the position at the first target, move the stop to break‑even on the remaining half, and let the rest run to a second target or trail with a moving average or ATR‑based stop.  

7. **Post‑Trade Review**  
   - After the trade closes (either by stop or target), record: entry, exit, profit/loss, reason for entry, any deviations from the plan, and observations about market context (breadth, sector rotation).  
   - Review the trade weekly to refine screener parameters, EMA lengths, or volume multipliers based on performance statistics.  

By following these steps, you convert the abstract concepts of breadth, rotation, screening, pattern recognition, momentum, and risk into a concrete, executable routine that can be run each trading day.

## Key Insights & Takeaways  
- **Start with market breadth**: a strong A/D Line and high % of stocks above key moving averages provide a favorable backdrop for long swing trades; weakening breadth signals caution or a shift to short‑biased setups.  
- **Use RRG to identify leading sectors**: sectors in the “leading” quadrant (strong relative strength and improving momentum) are the most likely sources of sustained price moves; monitor transitions from “improving” to “leading” for early entry.  
- **Combine screeners with technical patterns**: a screener that filters for trend, momentum, volume, and breakout conditions drastically reduces the universe to a manageable watchlist where you can then apply VCP, Darvas, or classic breakout rules for precise entries.  
- **Validate entries with momentum/EMA alignment**: ensure that short‑term momentum (e.g., 9‑EMA > 21‑EMA) and RSI are supportive before committing capital; this reduces false breakouts.  
- **Risk‑manage every trade**: calculate position size based on a fixed % of equity at risk, place stops at logical technical levels (below swing lows, box bottoms, or contraction lows), and use predefined targets or trailing stops to lock in gains.  
- **Scale out to optimize risk‑reward**: taking partial profits at the first target lets you lock in gains while allowing a portion of the position to capture larger moves, improving overall expectancy.  
- **Maintain a trade journal**: recording entry/exit rationale, market context, and post‑trade analysis enables continuous refinement of screener parameters, EMA lengths, and volume thresholds.  
- **Avoid overtrading**: limit concurrent positions and enforce a daily loss ceiling to preserve capital and psychological discipline.  
- **Leverage automation**: save screener templates, set pre‑market alerts, and use bracket orders to execute trades consistently without emotional interference.  
- **Continuously monitor sector rotation**: even after entering a trade, watch the RRG for signs of the sector weakening; consider exiting or tightening stops if a sector rotates out of the “leading” quadrant.
