## Core Concepts  

### Momentum Trading  
Momentum trading is a strategy that seeks to profit from the persistence of price trends. The core premise is that securities which have shown strong recent performance—typically measured over the past 3 to 12 months—are more likely to continue rising than to reverse. This effect is driven by investor behavior such as herding, delayed reactions to news, and institutional buying pressures. Practitioners often use relative strength rankings, moving average crossovers, or breakout signals to identify candidates.  

A key component of momentum trading within the IBD CAN SLIM framework is the “M” for Momentum, which requires a stock to show at least a 20‑25% increase in price over the last three months, accompanied by strong quarterly earnings growth. Momentum is not pursued in isolation; it is combined with fundamentals (earnings, sales growth, return on equity) and technicals (chart patterns, volume). This multi‑factor approach reduces the risk of buying into a fleeting rally that lacks underlying support.  

Effective momentum trading also demands strict risk management. Because trends can reverse abruptly, traders employ stop‑loss orders placed just below recent swing lows or below the base’s low to limit downside. Position sizing is adjusted based on volatility, ensuring that no single trade can jeopardize a significant portion of capital. The discipline to exit when momentum wanes—rather than hoping for a reversal—is what separates successful momentum traders from those who suffer large drawdowns.  

### Base Reset  
A base is a chart pattern that forms after a stock has experienced a significant advance and then consolidates, typically moving sideways within a defined range. Common base types include the cup‑with‑handle, flat base, and double bottom. The base represents a period where weak holders are shaken out and stronger hands accumulate shares, setting the stage for the next upward move.  

A base reset occurs when the stock’s price action violates the integrity of the existing base. This can happen in several ways: the price falls below the lowest point of the base (breakdown), fails to rebound from the base’s support level after a test, or exhibits excessive volatility that distorts the pattern’s shape. When a base resets, the prior consolidation no longer serves as a reliable launchpad, and any breakout from that pattern is suspect.  

Recognizing a base reset is crucial because it prevents traders from entering a position based on outdated technical structure. If a trader buys a stock thinking it is breaking out of a valid base, but the base has actually been reset, the trade is more likely to fail or produce a whipsaw. Conversely, identifying a reset early allows the trader to either avoid the stock, wait for a new base to form, or look for alternative setups with intact structures.

## How It Works / Step‑by‑Step  

### Step 1: Screen for Momentum Candidates  
Begin by applying a momentum filter to your watchlist. Using a platform such as TradingView, Thinkorswim, or a broker’s scanner, select stocks that have gained at least 20‑25% in price over the last 12 weeks. Add a fundamental filter requiring quarterly earnings per share (EPS) growth of 25% or more year‑over‑year and sales growth of at least 20%. This mirrors the “C” (Current quarterly earnings) and “A” (Annual earnings) components of CAN SLIM.  

### Step 2: Examine the Chart for a Base Pattern  
Open the daily chart of each candidate and look for a consolidation pattern that has lasted at least 7 weeks (the minimum for a proper base in IBD methodology). Identify the boundaries of the base: the highest point (top of the base) and the lowest point (base low). Note any classic formations such as a cup‑with‑handle, flat base, or double bottom.  

### Step 3: Validate the Base Integrity  
Check that the stock has not violated the base’s low since the pattern began. If the price has closed below the base low on a closing basis, the base is considered reset. Additionally, watch for signs of weakness: low volume on attempts to rebound from the base low, or a series of lower highs forming within the base range. These indicate that the base structure is deteriorating.  

### Step 4: Wait for a Breakout with Volume Confirmation  
A valid breakout occurs when the stock closes above the top of the base with volume at least 40‑50% above its 50‑day average volume. This surge in volume confirms institutional interest and reduces the likelihood of a false breakout. If the breakout occurs on weak volume, treat it with caution and consider waiting for a retest of the breakout level.  

### Step 5: Set Entry, Stop‑Loss, and Target  
Enter the trade near the breakout price or on a slight pullback to the breakout level. Place an initial stop‑loss just below the base low (or a few percent below the breakout point if the base is tight). Define a profit target based on the height of the base (e.g., aim for a gain equal to the base’s width) or use a trailing stop to let winners run.  

### Step 6: Monitor for Base Reset Signals During the Trade  
Even after entry, continue to watch for any signs that the base is being undermined. If the price falls back below the breakout level or shows weakening momentum (e.g., declining relative strength index), consider tightening the stop or exiting the position. This ongoing vigilance ensures that you remain aligned with the underlying technical structure.

## Common Pitfalls / What to Watch Out For  
- Chasing momentum without verifying base integrity often leads to buying into a stock that has already reset its base, resulting in whipsaw losses.  
- Ignoring volume on a breakout can cause traders to enter false breakouts that lack institutional backing and quickly reverse.  
- Setting stop‑losses too tight (e.g., within the base’s normal fluctuation range) may prematurely exit a trade that is still within a healthy consolidation phase.  
- Overlooking the time dimension—bases that are too short (less than 7 weeks) are considered unreliable and should be disregarded even if price action looks promising.  
- Failing to adjust position size for volatility can expose the portfolio to outsized losses when a momentum stock experiences a sudden spike in price swings.  
- Relying solely on price momentum while neglecting fundamental growth can lead to investing in companies with weak earnings prospects, increasing the risk of trend reversal when fundamentals deteriorate.  
- Allowing emotions to dictate exit decisions after a base reset signal—such as hoping for a reversal—often exacerbates losses; disciplined adherence to stop‑loss rules is essential.  
- Using a one‑size‑fits‑all base low stop‑loss without considering the specific base width may result in stops that are either too loose (excessive risk) or too tight (premature exits).  
- Neglecting to review past trades for patterns of base reset misidentification hinders learning; regular journal review is crucial for continuous improvement.
